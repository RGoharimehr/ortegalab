Advances in Liquid Crystal Transient Thermal Imaging

Project Team


Alfonso Ortega and Luis A. Tapia

Project Sponsor


Honeywell Engines & Systems

Motivation

 

Thermochromic Liquid Crystals (TLC), an organic substance, reflect light wavelength according to the temperature to which they are exposed. As a result, different colors are observed on a TLC covered surface, which in turn indicate the temperature of the surface. TLC's are therefore useful in determining the isotherms of an object. The information provided by TLC's can be further exploited to determine convective heat transfer coefficients on an article exposed to fluid flow.

Due to the characteristics described above, TLC thermography is widely used in thermal analysis of high temperature aircraft engine components such as turbine blades. In order to use TLC successfully, an imaging system, in this case a digital imaging system employing new technologies, capable of processing images and produce heat transfer coefficients is being developed.
 

 

Project Description


TTLC Calibration


An important aspect of the current project entails the calibration of TLC. From calibration, the relationship between color and temperature can be established. Due to the nature of the problem, the color space of interest is the Red-Green-Blue (RGB) space (Fig. 1).

Figure 1. RGB Color Space

  

 

Calibration of the TLC is performed by the use of a calibration unit (Fig. 2) driven by a PID controller that is in turn controlled by a computer system through LabVIEW (Fig. 3). During a calibration, the PID controller sets the temperature of the TLC surface by comparing the set temperature to the process temperature, acquired by an embedded thermistor, and then outputs the power needed to drive a thermoelectric cooler (TEC). The TEC is a heat pump and in this application takes energy from the recirculating water and transfers it to the TLC surface. An IEEE 1394 camera takes RGB images of the TLC surface.

 

 

Figure 3. TLC Calibration Unit

 

 

 

Figure 3. TLC Calibration Instrumentation

 

 

The RGB component of interest for the experiment is G; thus, all calibrations are presented are for this component only. Different calibrations were performed to establish the effect of lighting conditions, lighting intensity, number of light sources and camera angle on the G component (Fig. 4).

A narrow band TLC was calibrated with a red start at 35°C and a bandwidth of 1°C, R35CW1. It was observed that the lighting intensity affected the green value magnitude; however, its relationship to temperature was not (Fig. 5). The green peak value, Gmax, which is of utmost importance in this study, seemed to occur at the same temperature. The other variables altered both the green value magnitude and the temperature relationship (Fig. 6); that is, the curves shifted in green values and also shifted in temperature. However, for all of these variations Gmax can be located in the range of 34.7°C-35.2°C which implies the corresponding Gmax temperature can be taken as 34.95°C ± 0.25°C for any of these conditions.

Experimental Technique


The imaging system utilizes a transient technique for determining the heat transfer coefficients. This entails exposing a test article of a known initial temperature to a sudden step rise in temperature (Fig. 7) of a convective nature and record RGB images of the TLC covered area during this transient event. From calibration of the TLC, the maximum green value, Gmax, attained is correlated to its corresponding temperature. The recorded images will then give the time, referenced from the start of the temperature step rise, transpired in order for a particular pixel to achieve this temperature, tmax. This information is used with the semi-infinite solid approximation to determine the heat transfer coefficient.

Experimental Apparatus

 
The experimental set up consist of a open wind tunnel in which air is pre-heated and blocked from entering the test section by an automated gate valve (Fig. 9). When an experiment is ran, hot air is then allowed to flow in to the test section where an object is lighted by halogen lamps. The images are then recorded via an IEEE 1394 camera. Thermocouples in the test chamber record the gas temperature through National Instruments equipment and software.

Figure 8. Wind Tunnel

 

 

Imaging-Processing Software

 
The imaging software developed is built in National Instruments LabVIEW with the additional IMAQ and NI Vision add-on tool kits. The system is capable of acquiring images at different frame rates, play them back, process the image to look for Gmax and tmax for every pixel for a user-selected area, and determine h.
